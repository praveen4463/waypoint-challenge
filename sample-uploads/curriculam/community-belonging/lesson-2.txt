Knowledge focus This lesson's skill focus
Building on Lesson 1's definition of community as a "shared identity-forming
narrative," this lesson examines belonging as a personal, cultivable
experience. Students consider concrete strategies for building belonging in
their own lives, and analyze how an informational author uses text structure
(claim → reasons → strategies) to organize advice for a reader.
In this lesson, students practice
analyzing how an author organizes
an informational text and
determining how the structure
contributes to the development of
ideas. [RI.7.5]
Notes to Teachers

This lesson reinforces Lesson 1's definition of community by shifting from "what is community?" to "how do I belong to

shorter than Lesson 1's but uses a list-based informational structure that some students find harder to read for

About this Text
Kendra Cherry, MSEd, is a psychosocial rehabilitation specialist and an educational consultant. She writes about
psychology topics for general audiences. The following text is adapted from her informational article on belonging.
Purpose for Reading Vocabulary
To learn what psychologists mean by "a sense of belonging,"
and to understand specific actions a person can take to feel
more connected to a community.
Let's pronounce these words together as a class:
Cultivate [kuhl-tuh-veyt]
Initiate [ih-nish-ee-eyt]
Reciprocity [res-uh-pros-i-tee]
Vulnerable [vuhl-ner-uh-buhl]
WHOLE CLASS READING DURING READING QUESTIONS
[1] We all want to feel like we belong. The need to belong is one of the most
fundamental human needs, right alongside food, shelter, and safety. When we
feel like we belong, we are healthier, happier, and more resilient. When we feel
left out, we suffer — not just emotionally, but physically. Studies show that the
pain of social rejection activates the same parts of the brain as physical pain.
[2] But here is the good news: a sense of belonging is not something you either
have or don't have. It is something you can build. Belonging is a skill. Like any
skill, it can be practiced, strengthened, and grown over time. The strategies
below describe specific, research-supported ways to cultivate1 a stronger sense
of belonging in your own life.
Paragraphs 1-2
A. Think & Share: What does Cherry mean
when she says "belonging is a skill"?
●
She means it's not something fixed. It's
something you can practice and get
better at over time.
*B. Write: What does Cherry use paragraph 2
to do, in relation to paragraph 1?
●
Paragraph 1 says belonging matters.
Paragraph 2 says belonging can be built.
Paragraph 2 sets up the rest of the article,
which will explain how to build it.
PARTNER READING DURING READING QUESTIONS
[3] Strategy 1: Take small social risks. Belonging starts with small acts of
showing up. Say hello to someone new at school. Sit with a different group at
lunch once a week. Ask a classmate a question about something they are
working on. These actions feel risky because rejection is possible — but most of
the time, the response is positive. Each small risk that goes well teaches your
brain that connection is safe.
[4] Strategy 2: Initiate, 2 don't wait. People often assume that others should
reach out to them first. But everyone is waiting. If you wait for an invitation, you
may wait a long time. Inviting someone to study, sit together, or join an activity
is one of the most direct ways to build belonging. The act of initiating signals
that you value the relationship.
[5] Strategy 3: Practice reciprocity. 3 Healthy belonging is a two-way street.
Reciprocity means giving as much as you receive. Listen as much as you talk.
Show up for others' games, performances, and small moments — not only your
own. Communities form around mutual care, not one-sided connection.
[6] Strategy 4: Be vulnerable, 4 in small ways. Real belonging requires
allowing others to see who you actually are. This does not mean sharing
everything with everyone. It means letting trusted people see something honest
— a hope, a worry, a mistake you made. Vulnerability invites others to be real
with you in return, and that is the foundation of belonging.
Paragraphs 3-6:
A. Write: What is the same about how Cherry
introduces each strategy?
●
Each one starts with the word "Strategy"
and a number, then names the strategy in
a few words, then explains it.
B. Turn & Talk: Which of these four strategies
do you think would be hardest for a 7th grader,
and why?
●
Student responses will vary. Students
should reference one strategy and one
specific reason.
1 Cultivate (verb): to grow or develop something through care and effort
2 Initiate (verb): to start something
3 Reciprocity (noun): a fair exchange of giving and receiving between people
4 Vulnerable (adjective): willing to show emotion or take a risk that could lead to being hurt
WHOLE CLASS READING DURING READING QUESTIONS
[7] Strategy 5: Find your "one." Research on belonging shows that you do not
need to feel deeply connected to many people to feel like you belong. You need
one or two close, reliable relationships. Look for the person who notices when
you are quiet. Look for the person who remembers what you said last week. A
single secure connection is more powerful than many shallow ones.
[8] Strategy 6: Choose communities that match who you are becoming.
Lesson 1 of this unit defined community as a group of people who share an
identity-forming narrative. That definition matters here. The communities you
join shape the person you become. Choose communities — clubs, teams, online
groups, friend groups — whose stories about themselves match who you want
to be. Step away from communities whose stories do not.
Why does this matter?
[9] Belonging is built, not given. The strategies above are not magic. They
require effort, repeated attempts, and the courage to try again after a
disappointment. But every small act of reaching out, every honest moment, and
every reciprocal kindness builds the muscle of belonging — until one day you
realize you are no longer wondering whether you belong, because you are
already in it.
Paragraphs 7-9:
A. Turn & Talk: How does Strategy 6 connect
back to Lesson 1's reading by Toby Lowe?
●
Lowe defined community as a group with a
shared identity-forming narrative. Cherry
uses that idea to advise students to choose
communities whose stories fit who they
want to become.
B. Write: In your own words, explain why
Cherry organizes her article as a numbered list
of strategies rather than as a single paragraph
of advice.
●
A numbered list is easy to follow and lets
the reader hold each strategy as a
separate idea. It also signals that each
strategy is a separate, actionable step the
reader can try.
Find Evidence: Highlight one sentence from
paragraphs 7–9 that summarizes the central
idea of the whole article.
●
"Belonging is built, not given." (paragraph
9) — student answers may vary, accept
any sentence that captures the central
"belonging is a skill / built, not given"
idea.
